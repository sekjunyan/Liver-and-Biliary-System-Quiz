Short Answer Questions

These questions mirror the depth expected in a final-year clinical exam. Write your answer before revealing the model answer. Cover key points rather than reproducing verbatim.

Q1

A 7-year-old male-neutered Golden Retriever presents with a 2-week history of lethargy, icterus, and intermittent vomiting. Abdominal ultrasound reveals a massively enlarged, solitary liver mass in the left lateral lobe with a gravity-independent echogenic pattern in the gallbladder. List the preoperative investigations you would perform and the specific corrective action for each abnormal finding anticipated.

(12 marks)

Model Answer

Haematology & Coagulation:

  • PT/aPTT/PIVKA — vitamin K₁ 0.5–1.5 mg/kg SC/IM q12h × 3 doses if elevated; FFP 10 mL/kg if actively coagulopathic.
  • Platelet count — if <80,000/µL, major haemorrhage risk; delay biopsy/surgery.
  • PCV/TP — cross-match blood and have packed RBCs available.

Biochemistry:

  • Serum albumin — if <20 g/L give colloid (hetastarch 10–20 mL/kg in dogs).
  • Bilirubin, ALP, ALT, GGT — quantifies degree of cholestasis; markedly elevated values indicate friable parenchyma.
  • Blood glucose — supplement perioperatively if hypoglycaemic; monitor closely intraoperatively.
  • BUN, creatinine — EHBO can cause acute renal failure via endotoxin-mediated vasoconstriction.

Imaging:

  • Thoracic radiographs — screen for pulmonary metastases (massive HCC has excellent prognosis if no mets).
  • CT angiography — delineate vascular anatomy and confirm resectability.

Concurrent gallbladder finding: The gravity-independent echogenic pattern = gallbladder mucocoele. Confirm CBD patency at surgery. Plan concurrent cholecystectomy; elective mortality ~2% but rises to ~20% if ruptured.

Anaesthetic preparation: Prefer isoflurane/sevoflurane; avoid halothane. Prepare for ventilation (large hepatic mass may compress diaphragm). Clip cranial thorax bilaterally in case paracostal extension or thoracotomy is needed.

Q2

Describe the pathophysiology of extrahepatic biliary obstruction (EHBO) and explain how each systemic consequence should influence perioperative management of the patient.

(10 marks)

Model Answer

  • Cardiovascular (hypotension, ↓CO): "Jaundiced heart" — impaired response to vasopressors. Manage: aggressive isotonic crystalloid resuscitation (Normosol-R / PlasmaLyte preferred); avoid lactated Ringer's if severe hepatic dysfunction. Monitor blood pressure continuously.
  • Acute renal failure: Endotoxin → renal vasoconstriction → tubular necrosis. Manage: ensure adequate preoperative hydration; monitor urine output intraoperatively; avoid nephrotoxic drugs.
  • Coagulopathy / DIC: Vitamin K malabsorption (fat-soluble) → deficiency of factors II, VII, IX, X. Manage: Vit K₁ SC/IM pre-op; FFP if coagulopathic; monitor PT/aPTT/PIVKA.
  • GI haemorrhage: Endotoxin → gastric mucosal ischaemia + ↑ acid secretion. Manage: omeprazole/famotidine perioperatively.
  • Endotoxaemia: Bile absent from gut → ↑ gram-negative bacteria → endotoxin absorbed. Manage: broad-spectrum antibiotics (cefoxitin ± ampicillin); collect intraoperative bile cultures.
  • Delayed wound healing: Manage: minimise tissue trauma (Halsted's principles); use omentum to support biopsy/anastomosis sites.
  • Ultrasound timing: CBD dilates at 24–48 h; lobar ducts at 4–6 days; intrahepatic ducts by 1 week. Normal CBD = 3–4 mm. Sequential US helps confirm obstruction and guide timing of surgery.
Q3

Compare and contrast the guillotine, suture compression/wedge, and tissue fracture biopsy techniques of the liver. For each, describe the technique, ideal indication, and the key risk or limitation.

(9 marks)

Model Answer

Guillotine: Absorbable suture passed through liver parenchyma encircling vessels/bile ducts; tissue distal to ligature is excised. Ideal for pointed lobe ends. Key risk: ligature slippage — do NOT use for lobectomy due to risk of catastrophic haemorrhage if the suture fails on a large vessel.

Suture compression / Wedge: V-shaped suture lines placed along lobe edges to compress and isolate tissue; excised sharply within the V. Ideal for peripheral, edge lesions. Key risk: minimal if technique is correct, but requires accessible lobe margins; not suitable for deep/central lesions.

Tissue fracture: Blunt fracture of parenchyma with scalpel handle or fingers; reveals intact vessels and bile ducts that are then individually clamped and ligated. Ideal for larger masses where individual vessel ligation is needed. Key risk: parenchymal surface bleeding, which must be controlled with topical haemostatic agents and individual ligation — requires meticulous technique.

Q4

A 3-year-old male Yorkshire Terrier presents with stunted growth, post-prandial ptyalism, and a single episode of seizure-like activity. Bile acids are markedly elevated. Describe your medical stabilisation protocol prior to surgery and explain why each element is important.

(10 marks)

Model Answer

Minimum 2 weeks of medical stabilisation is required before anaesthesia for PSS.

  • Lactulose (0.5–1.0 mL/kg PO q6–8h) — osmotically traps NH₃ in colon; acidifies colonic environment; targets 2–3 soft stools/day. Reduces ammonia load causing HE.
  • Antibiotics — metronidazole (7.5 mg/kg q12h) or ampicillin (22 mg/kg q6h) — reduce urease-producing intestinal bacteria, decreasing ammonia production.
  • Dietary protein restriction — 18–22% DM basis in dogs; dairy/vegetable proteins preferred (fewer aromatic amino acids). Prevents excess ammonia generation while maintaining nutritional support.
  • IV fluid therapy — electrolyte and glucose support (especially in young/small patients at risk of hypoglycaemia); corrects dehydration and metabolic disturbances.
  • GI protection — famotidine or omeprazole, especially for intrahepatic PSS (30% GI ulceration preoperatively). Continue lifelong post-attenuation.
  • Hepatoprotectants — SAMe, ursodeoxycholic acid, Vit E, milk thistle — limited evidence but commonly used to support hepatocellular function.
  • Anticonvulsants — if ongoing seizure risk: leuprolide, KBr, or phenobarbitone (noting hepatic metabolism of phenobarb).
  • Note on pRBCs/FFP: stored pRBCs can accumulate ammonia — use with caution; FFP provides coagulation factors if coagulopathic.
Q5

Explain why cholecystoduodenostomy is preferred over cholecystojejunostomy as a biliary diversion procedure, and describe the key technical considerations for performing a cholecystoduodenostomy, including the minimum stoma size and how the species anatomy of the cat affects technique selection.

(8 marks)

Model Answer

Why duodenum is preferred: Bile entering the duodenum maintains the physiological feedback loop — cholecystokinin is released, pancreatic secretion is stimulated, and gastric acid is neutralised by bile salts. Bypassing the duodenum (jejunostomy) disrupts this feedback and risks gastric ulceration due to loss of acid-neutralising bile.

Minimum stoma size: >2.5 cm — critical to prevent post-operative stricture formation and re-obstruction.

Technical steps: Confirm CBD is patent (or bypassed) before diversion; appose gallbladder wall to anti-mesenteric border of duodenum without tension; full-thickness anastomosis with absorbable suture; verify patency by retrograde flushing.

Dog vs cat anatomy: In dogs, the CBD and pancreatic duct enter the duodenum separately — a cholecystoduodenostomy effectively bypasses the obstructed CBD while pancreatic drainage remains independent. In cats, the CBD and pancreatic duct conjoin before the major duodenal papilla; only ~20% of cats have an accessory pancreatic duct. This means obstruction at the papilla in cats affects both biliary AND pancreatic drainage simultaneously. For cats with inflammatory obstruction (e.g. from pancreatitis), choledochal stenting is often preferable as it is reversible — but cats have higher re-obstruction rates (2/7 within 1 week) vs dogs. For permanent diversion, the conjoined anatomy in cats does not preclude cholecystoduodenostomy but complicates the underlying disease management.

Q6

A 9-year-old Shetland Sheepdog presents with acute abdominal pain, mild jaundice, and a leukocytosis. Ultrasound shows a stellate pattern in the gallbladder with pericholecystic hyperechoic fat and a small volume of free abdominal fluid. Effusion bilirubin is 4× serum bilirubin. Outline your immediate management and surgical plan.

(10 marks)

Model Answer

Diagnosis: Ruptured gallbladder mucocoele with bile peritonitis (effusion:serum bilirubin ≥2:1 confirms bile peritonitis; stellate pattern = mucocoele; pericholecystic fat + free fluid = rupture).

Immediate stabilisation:

  • Aggressive IV crystalloid (Normosol-R or PlasmaLyte) — isotonic fluid resuscitation at 30–60 mL/kg/hr; titrate to response.
  • Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics immediately — cefoxitin 15 mg/kg IV TID ± ampicillin 22 mg/kg IV TID for Enterococcus coverage.
  • Coagulation support: Vit K₁ SC/IM; FFP 10 mL/kg if coagulopathic; colloid (hetastarch) if albumin <20 g/L.
  • Analgesia (opioids; avoid NSAIDs in jaundiced/renally compromised patient).
  • Blood type and crossmatch; secure blood products.

Surgical plan (once haemodynamically stable):

  • Midline celiotomy (xyphoid to pubis).
  • Cholecystectomy — treatment of choice; removes mucus-producing source.
  • Confirm CBD patency (retrograde catheterisation via duodenotomy; normograde flushing acceptable).
  • Thorough peritoneal lavage with warm sterile saline.
  • Collect bile and tissue for aerobic and anaerobic culture.
  • Closed-suction or open abdominal drainage (Jackson-Pratt) if significant peritoneal contamination remains.
  • Submit gallbladder for histopathology.

Prognosis: Non-elective (ruptured) mucocoele carries ~20% mortality vs ~2% elective. Albumin and ALT are key prognostic indicators. Discuss this with the owner.
